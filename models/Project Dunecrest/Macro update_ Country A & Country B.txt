Macro: Country A Update_ Key Rating Drivers & Outlook (rating-agency report, Aug 2026)
Kessport Frontier Managers Limited

Macro update   |   3
Country A update
External position and financing
I Ratings upgraded on an improved external position: the one-notch upgrade to B with a Stable outlook reflects a current-account deficit that has narrowed to 2.1% of GDP in 2025 from an average of 5.9%
over 2019-24, a rebuilding of reserves to USD 7.9 billion and an orderly repayment record. The rating remains constrained by the absence of international market access since 2023, the lack of a
programme with a multilateral lender, a high vulnerability to imported energy and grain prices, and the still-pending reform of energy and food subsidies.
I Lower current-account deficits, widening from a low base: the agency projects the deficit at 2.7% of GDP in 2026 and 3.1% in 2027 as import demand recovers with growth. The services balance has
improved on tourism receipts of about 7.1% of GDP and remittance inflows of 4.9% of GDP, both above their 2019 levels. The projected deficits remain well below the 3.4% of GDP median for single-B
rated peers, which supports the external assessment.
I Resilient external financing: net FDI reached 2.1% of GDP in 2025 against a ten-year average of 1.8%, concentrated in export manufacturing, renewable energy and pharmaceuticals. Multilateral and
bilateral disbursements of about USD 1.1 billion are expected in 2026, covering roughly half of gross external financing needs. The agency expects FDI to rebound further to 2.5% of GDP in 2027 as
announced industrial projects reach financial close.
I Narrowing net external financing outflows: external amortisations decline from 2.9% of GDP in 2025 to 2.4% in 2026 and 2.0% in 2027. The remaining international bond, USD 420 million maturing in
March 2028, is the last commercial external maturity and is expected to be met from reserves and a further drawing under the stabilisation facility. Reserves are equivalent to 3.9 months of current external
payments, up from 3.2 months at end-2023, and are projected at 4.2 months by end-2027.
Fiscal and debt dynamics
I High but declining fiscal financing needs: gross fiscal financing needs fall from 12.1% of GDP in 2025 to 10.9% in 2026 and 9.8% in 2027, against a 2019-24 average of 10.3% and a peer median of 8.2%.
External amortisations account for roughly a third of the total, with the remainder rolled over domestically through the banking system and the treasury-bill market at maturities that have lengthened
modestly over the past year.
I Domestic stabilisation facility: under the Treasury Stabilisation Facility agreed with the central bank, the bank subscribed LCA 4.5 billion of long-dated treasury paper (about USD 1.1 billion at the model's
USD 0.235 per LCA conversion rate) at a concessional 1.0% coupon with a 12-year tenor and a two-year grace period, covering a large part of the 2025 domestic financing need. The domestic financing
need is projected to decline from 7.9% of GDP in 2025 to 6.6% in 2027. The agency assumes a further drawing of similar structure to service the March 2028 bond, which it views as credit-neutral given
reserve adequacy.
I Declining fiscal deficits: the overall deficit narrows from 5.8% of GDP in 2025 to 5.2% in 2026 and 4.7% in 2027, driven by containment of the wage bill at about 13.6% of GDP despite the resumption of
civil-service hiring, and by a decline in subsidy costs from 2.9% to 2.2% of GDP on lower imported energy prices. The primary deficit improves from 1.5% to 0.4% of GDP over the same period; revenue
measures contribute only marginally.
I High public debt: general government debt is stable at 78.3% of GDP in 2025, of which external debt is 40.9% and domestic debt 37.4%; the agency projects 78.2% in 2026 and 77.8% in 2027. The small
decline is attributable to the currency's cumulative appreciation since 2023 reducing the local-currency value of external debt and to lower primary deficits, rather than to nominal growth, which remains
below the effective interest rate on domestic debt.

Country B update
1. The recovery since the 2020 external shock has been driven by hydrocarbon revenues and public spending.
Real GDP grew 3.1% in 2025 after 3.2% in 2024, supported by a fiscal expansion under the multi-year public
investment plan that lifted public wages, transfers and capital spending. The overall fiscal deficit widened to 10.2%
of GDP in 2025 from 4.8% in 2023, while inflation eased markedly on lower food prices and a firmer nominal
exchange rate.
2. The government's medium-term programme implies policy continuity. The 2026 budget prioritises household
purchasing power, public investment in transport and water infrastructure, and diversification beyond
hydrocarbons. Independent forecasters continue to argue that durable diversification requires private-sector-led
structural reform: a more open investment regime, a smaller public footprint in banking and industry, and a
streamlined land-allocation process.
3. Real GDP growth tapered while inflation fell sharply. Hydrocarbon output contracted 0.7% in 2025 as the state
producer's output ceiling and field maintenance limited crude volumes, whereas the non-hydrocarbon economy
grew 4.4% on construction, agriculture and public services. Average inflation fell to 3.9% in 2025 from 7.4% in
2023, with fresh-food prices contributing 0.9 percentage points against 2.5 points two years earlier.
4. The current account turned to a deficit of 1.8% of GDP in 2025 (surplus of 7.4% in 2022) on lower hydrocarbon
volumes and prices and higher capital-goods imports. The nominal effective exchange rate was broadly stable; the
local currency (LCB) has weakened 3.5% against the US dollar over two years to 80.0 LCB per USD (USD 0.0125
per LCB, the model rate). Gross reserves stood at USD 53.7 billion, or 11.3 months of imports.
5. Banks are liquid, solvent and profitable, but non-performing loans remain high at about 15% of gross loans,
concentrated in state-owned enterprises. Credit to the economy grew 7.4% in 2025, with credit to the private sector
up 5.6% while net credit to government expanded 12.9% as the treasury drew on domestic bank financing to cover
the widening deficit. Capital adequacy averages 18.2% and the loan-to-deposit ratio is about 75%.
